# Recipient Profile Intake

Use this guide when recipient-profile evidence includes chat screenshots, annotated reports, meeting notes, concrete behavior examples, or questionnaire answers. The purpose is to infer report-relevant preferences for user confirmation, not to assess the recipient's personality.

## Evidence sources

Useful sources can include, but are not limited to:

- the user's spoken or written description of the recipient;
- WeChat or other chat screenshots supplied by the user;
- comments, tracked changes, or annotations on earlier reports;
- meeting notes or the user's account of verbal feedback;
- examples of material the recipient praised, questioned, shortened, expanded, accepted, or returned for revision;
- repeated questions the recipient asks during research or work updates;
- recipient-authored templates or instructions.

Peer templates may inform report style at their lower authority level. They do not establish recipient-profile rules.

## When to start and stop inquiry

Apply [the recipient-profile inquiry conditions in SKILL.md](../SKILL.md#recipient-profile-inquiry-conditions) before asking questions from this guide. Inspecting evidence or drafting a profile does not itself trigger an interview. Respect upload completion, the user's inquiry preference, and any applicable inquiry-preference gate. Reuse sufficient evidence and confirmed rules; ask only about consequential gaps or unresolved conflicts, unless the user explicitly requests broader profile exploration. Stop when the decision is supported, the user declines, or they do not know; keep remaining preferences unknown.

## Heuristic workflow

1. Inventory the evidence and identify its date, context, author, and relationship to a specific report when available.
2. Extract observable behavior without adding motive. Examples: `asked for a baseline three times`, `moved the conclusion to the first paragraph`, or `requested a concrete owner and date for the next action`.
3. Convert each observation into one or more narrow report-relevant hypotheses.
4. Record plausible alternative explanations. A request may be project-specific, caused by missing information, or reflect a stable preference.
5. Assign confidence and scope using the rubric below.
6. Apply the start/skip/stop conditions above; when inquiry is appropriate, ask only about unresolved preferences whose answers would change report decisions or the profile exploration explicitly requested by the user.
7. Present the draft profile and supporting interpretation to the user for correction and confirmation.

Use this reasoning shape:

| Observable behavior | Candidate report implication | Alternative explanation | Scope | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Repeatedly asks what baseline was used | Put comparisons and baselines near each major result | Current project may have had an unusually weak baseline | cross-report candidate | medium |

Do not present the candidate implication as a fact about the recipient until the user confirms it.

## Screenshot handling

- Inspect only screenshots the user supplies for this task.
- Identify which messages belong to the recipient, the user, and other participants. Ask one clarifying question when authorship materially changes the interpretation and the inquiry conditions permit it; otherwise exclude the ambiguous material from profile evidence.
- Preserve enough surrounding context to understand what prompted a reply; do not interpret an isolated message without its relevant prompt when the meaning depends on that prompt.
- Extract report-relevant observations and source locations. Do not copy unrelated conversation into the profile.
- Exclude contact names, account identifiers, avatars, phone numbers, group-member details, unrelated messages, and other personal information that does not affect report adaptation.
- Treat OCR uncertainty as uncertainty. Mark unreadable or ambiguous text instead of guessing.
- Do not infer preferences from response latency, online status, punctuation, emoji use, message length, politeness, or social warmth unless the recipient explicitly connects that behavior to reporting expectations.

## Adaptive questionnaire mode

Offer questionnaire mode when the user requests it or prefers to answer several independent questions together. Keep it compact and adapt it to evidence already available. Do not ask for facts already established by supplied material.

Questions should request concrete behavior examples. Useful modules include:

### Attention and opening

- Think of a report the recipient considered effective. What did they respond to first: the result, the process, the risk, or the next decision?
- What information do they commonly ask to move earlier?
- What do they often skip, shorten, or call unnecessary?

### Evidence and evaluation

- When you report progress, what proof do they usually ask for: numbers, comparisons, reproducibility, milestones, practical impact, or something else?
- What makes them say that a task is complete rather than merely in progress?
- Which claims tend to trigger follow-up questions, and what additional evidence resolves them?

### Failure, risk, and next steps

- When a result is negative or a task is blocked, what response do they prefer: cause analysis, alternatives, a recovery plan, a decision request, or another form?
- How specific do owners, dates, milestones, or resource needs need to be?
- Which risks do they want surfaced immediately, and which can wait for the normal weekly report?

### Feedback examples

- What have they repeatedly praised in previous reports?
- What have they repeatedly asked you to revise or remove?
- Can you provide one or two exact comments, screenshots, or before-and-after examples that show this pattern?

Tell the user that every question is optional and that partial answers are useful. Allow free-form answers. When answer choices help recall, include them as examples rather than exhaustive personality categories.

## Confidence rubric

- **High**: explicit recipient instruction or a stable pattern supported by several clear examples across reports.
- **Medium**: repeated behavior with consistent context, or one clear feedback example plus user confirmation.
- **Low**: one ambiguous example, incomplete screenshot context, uncertain authorship, or an interpretation with plausible competing explanations.

Low-confidence hypotheses may guide a clarification question. Do not persist them as reusable rules until the user confirms them. Contradictory evidence lowers confidence and must be shown during profile confirmation.

## Confirmation view

Show the user a compact view separate from the weekly report:

| Proposed profile rule | Evidence | Confidence | Effect on future reports | User correction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | |

Group items as confirmed rules, proposed hypotheses, and meaningful unknowns. Ask whether the interpretation is accurate. Update the reusable profile only after confirmation.
